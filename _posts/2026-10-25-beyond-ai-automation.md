I've been thinking about what the next stage of working with AI should look like.

Most of what we're building today focuses on making AI do more work. It can write code, generate designs, analyze data, create marketing campaigns, and automate entire workflows.

But I think there's a more interesting problem to solve: **How do we design the way humans and AI work together to get the best outcome from both?**

Not just getting AI to do more. Not just keeping a human in the loop. But figuring out how their intelligence should interact throughout the process.

## The problem with how we use AI

Take building an application with Claude Code.

A developer describes what they want, AI writes the code, and the developer reviews the changes. The developer can approve, reject, or ask for modifications.

This is already a powerful way to build software. But it can also turn the developer into someone who supervises AI's work rather than actively shaping how the solution develops.

There's a difference between a developer who asks AI to build an application and reviews the result, and one who uses AI to challenge architectural decisions, explore alternative solutions, investigate unfamiliar problems, and rethink the implementation as new insights emerge.

Both are using AI. But they're using their intelligence differently within the process.

This isn't a criticism of tools like Claude Code. It's a question about how we design the work around them.

The same pattern appears in design, marketing, growth, and research. We give AI a task, receive an output, and review it. Sometimes we iterate several times, but the underlying relationship often remains the same: AI produces, and the human directs or evaluates.

What if we designed the process differently?

## Beyond assigning tasks to humans and AI

A common way to think about AI is to decide which tasks humans should perform and which AI should handle.

AI is good at certain kinds of analysis, generating alternatives, synthesizing information, and executing work at scale. Humans contribute experience, context, intuition, judgment, and an understanding of what matters in a particular situation.

So we divide the work accordingly.

But I think this misses something important: **the value isn't just in what each can do independently. It's in how one changes what the other can do next.**

Consider a growth team trying to understand why users are dropping off during onboarding.

AI analyzes the data and identifies a pattern. A growth practitioner looks at the same evidence and recognizes a possible reason: users might not understand why they need to connect their data before seeing value from the product.

That observation changes what the AI should investigate next. Further analysis might support the hypothesis, challenge it, or reveal something the team hadn't considered.

The new evidence changes the team's understanding, which creates new questions for the AI to explore.

The process isn't simply AI doing analysis and a human doing strategy. The human's interpretation changes the analysis, and the analysis changes the human's thinking.

That's the kind of interaction I'm interested in.

## Why this matters for design

The rise of vibe coding is an interesting example.

AI has made it much easier to build functional websites and applications from prompts. But being able to generate a website doesn't mean you've solved the design problem.

Give people access to the same tools, and many will produce functional results that rely on familiar layouts, patterns, and visual conventions. The ability to generate the output becomes less distinctive when more people can do it.

A strong designer brings something beyond the ability to produce an interface. They can recognize a weak idea, understand a user problem, challenge assumptions, and develop a direction that makes the product distinctive.

AI can help that designer explore more possibilities and build prototypes much faster. Those prototypes can, in turn, reveal possibilities the designer hadn't considered.

The designer's judgment changes what AI produces. The output changes what the designer sees as possible. The next iteration benefits from both.

That's more interesting to me than simply using AI to generate ten versions of a landing page and choosing the best one.

The question is whether we can design tools and processes that make this kind of interaction more effective, rather than leaving it entirely to how an individual happens to use AI.

## The same applies to growth

Growth is another useful example because it's easy to confuse production with progress.

AI can generate hundreds of campaign ideas, write landing-page copy, analyze conversion data, and suggest experiments.

But producing more experiments doesn't necessarily mean learning more about customers.

Imagine a team sees that users abandon onboarding at a particular step. AI can suggest changes to the page, the form, or the copy.

A person with a deeper understanding of the customer might recognize that the problem isn't the interface at all. Perhaps users don't yet understand the value they'll receive by completing that step.

That insight changes the problem being solved. The team can investigate whether it's true, use AI to analyze relevant data, and develop a different experiment based on what it learns.

The outcome depends on more than AI's ability to generate ideas or the human's ability to make decisions. It depends on how their contributions change the investigation over time.

Of course, this is an illustrative scenario, not evidence that such a process will always outperform a conventional growth workflow. The point is that it creates a different way to approach the problem.

## The goal isn't more human involvement

There's an important distinction here.

I'm not suggesting that humans should participate in every step, that AI should constantly ask questions, or that every task requires collaboration.

Some tasks are repetitive and well-defined. Automating them entirely may be the best option.

Other tasks are ambiguous, creative, or strategic. In those situations, human judgment might materially change the outcome, while AI can expand the possibilities a person can explore.

The appropriate balance should depend on the problem.

Nor does combining two sources of intelligence automatically make the result better. Humans can introduce bias, AI can produce misleading answers, and coordination has a cost. Sometimes a capable person working alone will outperform a human-AI combination.

That's why the goal shouldn't be to maximize human involvement or AI autonomy. It should be to maximize the quality of the outcome.

The more useful question is: **Where does the interaction between human and AI intelligence actually create value, and how can we design for it?**

## Is this a new way of thinking about AI?

I'm not claiming that human-AI collaboration is a new concept. Companies already build tools that let people and AI iterate together, and researchers study how to combine their capabilities.

What interests me is whether we can go further than assigning tasks, adding review steps, or putting AI into existing workflows.

Can we deliberately design how the two influence each other throughout a process?

Can we identify when a human's input would significantly improve the AI's direction, when AI could challenge a person's assumptions, and when either should work independently?

Could the process adapt as the problem changes, instead of keeping the same roles from beginning to end?

And, most importantly, can we demonstrate that this leads to better results than simply using existing AI tools well?

I don't think there's one universal answer. The right balance will differ between building software, designing products, conducting research, and growing a business.

But I suspect there's an opportunity in designing the interaction itself, rather than focusing exclusively on the capabilities of the individual human or AI.

## Beyond making AI do more

The first phase of AI adoption has understandably focused on what can be automated and how much faster work can get done.

The next opportunity may be to rethink how work is performed when both human and AI intelligence are available.

Not every task needs a human. Not every decision should be delegated to AI. And not every collaboration produces a better result.

But when each can meaningfully improve the other's contribution, the process should make that possible.

**Perhaps the question shouldn't just be, "What can AI do for us?" It should also be, "How should we work with AI so that the combination can achieve something better?"**

That's the idea I'm interested in exploring.
