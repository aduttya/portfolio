AI can now turn a prompt into a working interface. It can generate layouts, write frontend code, create prototypes, and iterate on designs in a fraction of the time it once took.

This changes the economics of building software. But it also raises a more interesting question: **when producing an interface becomes easier, what makes the resulting product better?**

I think the answer has less to do with how quickly a designer can generate screens and more to do with how they combine their own thinking with AI throughout the design process.

This is the product design side of an idea I explored more broadly in [Beyond AI Automation](/blog/beyond-ai-automation/), about how human and AI intelligence can change what each other does next. I apply the same thinking to growth in [AI for Growth](/blog/ai-for-growth/).

## The problem with AI-generated design

Vibe coding is a good example of what's changing.

Someone with an idea can use AI to build a functional website without having to master frontend development. A designer can explore more visual directions without spending hours implementing each one. A product manager can turn an idea into a prototype and test whether it makes sense.

These are real advantages.

But the ability to produce an interface doesn't mean the underlying product problem has been solved.

A website can look polished and still communicate the wrong message. An onboarding flow can be visually clean and still confuse users. A prototype can work perfectly while solving a problem nobody particularly cares about.

As more people gain access to similar production capabilities, I suspect that production alone will become less of a differentiator.

The difficult part isn't always making something. It's deciding what deserves to be made, understanding why it should exist, and determining whether the result actually works for the people using it.

## The designer's role isn't just to review AI output

One obvious way to incorporate AI into product design is to let it generate interfaces and have the designer choose the best option.

This is useful, especially when the alternative is producing every variation manually. But it creates a relatively narrow role for the designer: AI explores, and the human evaluates.

I don't think that is the most interesting possibility.

Consider a designer working on a SaaS onboarding experience. The initial assumption is that users are abandoning the process because it has too many steps.

AI can help analyze feedback, generate alternative flows, and rapidly prototype a simplified experience.

But what if the designer recognizes that the number of steps isn't the real problem? Users might be reluctant to connect their data because they haven't yet understood the product's value.

That changes the problem itself.

Instead of simply reducing steps, the designer might explore how to demonstrate value before asking users to commit. AI can prototype several approaches, expose trade-offs, and help investigate the consequences of each direction.

The resulting prototypes may reveal new problems or opportunities that change the designer's understanding again.

The process is no longer simply a designer giving instructions to AI or reviewing its output. The designer's thinking changes what AI explores, and AI's exploration changes what the designer considers possible.

This is an illustrative example, not a claim that this approach will always outperform a conventional design process. The point is to show where a more deliberate combination of human and AI intelligence could create value.

## Designing the interaction, not just the interface

This leads to a question that I think deserves more attention: how should we design the relationship between a designer and AI?

Not every stage needs the same balance.

At the beginning, the designer might need to understand the users, business constraints, and the actual problem. AI can help synthesize research and surface patterns, but the designer must critically evaluate whether those patterns matter.

During exploration, AI can generate alternatives and implement them quickly. The designer can challenge assumptions, recognize weak directions, and introduce ideas that redirect the exploration.

During validation, AI can help organize feedback and analyze results. The designer can interpret the context, identify unanswered questions, and decide what needs to change.

Sometimes the designer should lead. Sometimes AI should work independently. Sometimes the most valuable result comes from repeated interaction between the two.

The important decision isn't simply who performs each task. It's how their contributions influence what happens next.

That distinction matters because a process with many human review steps isn't necessarily better than one with fewer. Nor does giving AI more autonomy guarantee a better outcome.

The goal is to find the right combination for the problem.

## Taste is important, but it isn't enough

There's a growing argument that taste will become more valuable as AI makes production cheaper. I agree with part of it.

When generating a hundred design variations becomes easy, knowing which direction is worth pursuing matters more.

But taste shouldn't be reduced to choosing the prettiest interface from a collection of AI-generated options.

Product design also requires understanding user behavior, making trade-offs, identifying the right problem, and connecting design decisions to the product's objectives.

A designer's judgment matters most when it changes the direction of the work, not merely when it approves the final output.

At the same time, human judgment isn't infallible. Designers can become attached to their ideas, overlook evidence, and mistake personal preferences for user needs. AI can expose alternatives they would not have considered.

That's another reason to design for interaction rather than treating human creativity as inherently superior to AI generation.

The designer should be able to challenge AI, but AI should also be able to provide evidence or alternatives that challenge the designer.

Neither should be immune to revision.

## What happens to product differentiation?

Imagine two teams building similar products with access to comparable AI tools.

Both can generate interfaces quickly. Both can produce prototypes, write code, and explore common design patterns.

The first team uses AI primarily to accelerate implementation. The second uses it to investigate assumptions, explore different approaches, and repeatedly reconsider the problem as new evidence emerges.

The second team has an opportunity to reach a better result, not because it uses more AI, but because it uses AI to improve the quality of its exploration and decisions.

Of course, better processes don't guarantee better products. The team still needs good inputs, useful evidence, and sound judgment. The only way to establish whether the approach works is to evaluate the outcomes.

But this is where I see the opportunity. If production becomes cheaper for everyone, the quality of the process that guides production may matter more.

## What should product designers do differently?

I don't think the answer is to replace familiar design practices with AI-first workflows.

Instead, designers should question how their work changes when AI can participate in more of it.

Can AI help challenge the initial problem framing, rather than simply execute the brief?

Can a designer's observations redirect what AI explores, rather than only determine which generated option is best?

Can prototypes be used to change the team's understanding of the problem, rather than merely demonstrate an idea?

And can the process adjust as the team learns, allowing AI or the designer to take the lead where each contributes the most?

These questions are more useful than simply asking which design tasks can be automated.

They also require designers to evaluate AI critically. Generating more alternatives is not inherently useful, and involving a human at every step can make the process slower without improving the outcome.

The measure should be whether the process produces a better product, not how many AI features it contains.

## The opportunity in AI-era product design

AI is changing what designers can produce and how quickly they can produce it. That is already significant.

But the more interesting opportunity is to reconsider how the design process itself should work.

Rather than treating the designer as the person who gives AI instructions or reviews its output, we can design processes where human judgment and AI capabilities continuously shape one another.

Sometimes that will mean automating a task. Sometimes it will mean giving a designer more control. Sometimes it will mean allowing AI to challenge an assumption or take the exploration in an unexpected direction.

The balance will depend on the problem, and not every interaction will improve the result.

**The opportunity isn't just to use AI to design interfaces faster. It's to figure out how designers and AI can work together to make better product decisions and create better products.**
