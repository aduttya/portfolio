I've been thinking about how AI is changing growth work.

It's becoming easier to generate marketing copy, analyze conversion data, create landing pages, and come up with experiments. A growth practitioner can now explore ideas and execute them faster than before.

But I keep coming back to a question: **Does producing more growth experiments mean we're getting better at growth?**

I don't think it necessarily does.

Growth isn't just about executing experiments. It's about understanding why people behave the way they do, identifying opportunities, testing assumptions, and learning what actually moves a business forward.

AI can accelerate much of this work. The more interesting opportunity, to me, is figuring out how human and AI intelligence can improve each other throughout the process.

This is the growth side of an idea I started in [Beyond AI Automation](/blog/beyond-ai-automation/) and applied to design in [Product Design in the AI Era](/blog/product-design-in-the-ai-era/).

## The problem with generating more growth ideas

Imagine a SaaS company struggling to convert website visitors into signups.

AI can analyze analytics data, generate landing-page variations, suggest headlines, and propose experiments. A growth practitioner can use these capabilities to test more ideas in less time.

But what if the team is solving the wrong problem?

Perhaps the landing page isn't the issue. Visitors might understand the product perfectly well but simply not consider the problem important enough to act on. Or the page might attract people whose needs don't match what the product actually offers.

Testing another headline could improve conversion, but it might also distract the team from a more fundamental issue.

The difficulty isn't always a lack of ideas or execution capacity. Sometimes it's a lack of understanding about which problem deserves attention.

AI can help investigate that problem, but someone still needs to question the assumptions behind the investigation. And the human's assumptions should be open to challenge by what AI discovers, too.

## Where human and AI intelligence can improve each other

Consider a hypothetical example.

A growth team notices that visitors are reading a SaaS product's pricing page but aren't signing up.

AI analyzes the available data and identifies a pattern: visitors from one acquisition channel are much less likely to convert than visitors from another.

That observation alone doesn't explain why.

A growth practitioner might recognize that the first channel attracts people who are still exploring the problem, while the second brings people actively looking for a solution. It's a plausible explanation based on the practitioner's understanding of the audience, but it isn't yet a proven conclusion.

That insight gives AI a more specific question to investigate. The practitioner might ask for a comparison of the two audiences' behavior, the pages they visit, and the points where they leave.

The analysis could support the hypothesis, contradict it, or reveal another explanation.

Each result changes what the team should investigate next. If the original explanation is wrong, the practitioner needs to rethink the problem. If it holds up, the team can design an experiment that addresses the difference between the audiences rather than simply changing the pricing page for everyone.

This is the kind of interaction I find interesting.

AI brings patterns and analysis that may be difficult to uncover manually. Human understanding helps interpret what those patterns might mean. The interpretation redirects the analysis, and the new evidence changes the human's thinking.

The value isn't simply that AI performed analysis and a human made a decision. It's that each contribution changed the direction of the investigation.

Of course, this is an illustrative scenario, not evidence that this approach will always improve conversion. The hypothesis still needs to be tested, and observed patterns don't automatically establish causation.

## Growth is a learning problem

It's easy to measure growth activity: experiments launched, campaigns published, landing pages created, or hours saved through automation.

These metrics can be useful for understanding execution, but they don't necessarily tell me whether a team is getting better at growth.

A team could launch dozens of experiments and learn very little if the experiments are poorly designed or based on weak assumptions.

Another team might run fewer experiments but discover something important about its customers, positioning, or product experience.

The difference is often the quality of the questions being investigated.

AI can help generate hypotheses, analyze data, and explore possible explanations. But the ability to generate a hypothesis isn't the same as knowing whether it's worth testing. Nor does a convincing explanation mean the evidence supports it.

The growth process needs to distinguish between observations, hypotheses, and validated conclusions.

This is where I see room for better human-AI interaction. AI can challenge a practitioner's assumptions with data, while the practitioner can challenge AI's interpretation using customer knowledge and business context. Together, they can develop more useful questions and determine what evidence would help answer them.

The objective isn't to make AI agree with the growth practitioner or to make the practitioner defer to AI. It's to improve the quality of the reasoning that leads to the next decision.

## Why a fixed workflow isn't enough

A conventional growth workflow might assign data analysis to AI, experiment selection to a human, and implementation back to AI.

That's a reasonable way to organize work, and it can be effective.

But the best division of responsibilities can change as the team learns.

At one point, AI might be best positioned to explore a large dataset. At another, a growth practitioner's knowledge of the market might expose a flaw in the analysis. Later, an experiment's results might require the team to rethink the original problem entirely.

The process shouldn't force each participant to stay within a predefined role.

What interests me is whether growth tools can better support this changing relationship: helping people investigate the right questions, making it easier for human insights to redirect AI analysis, and using new evidence to determine what should happen next.

This goes beyond automating an existing workflow. It means paying attention to how the interaction between human and AI intelligence affects the quality of the work.

That doesn't mean every step needs both. A routine reporting task may be best automated. A high-impact positioning decision may require significant human judgment. The appropriate balance depends on the uncertainty, risk, and nature of the problem.

## What would I want from an AI growth tool?

I wouldn't judge a growth tool only by how many experiments it can generate or how much marketing work it automates.

I'd want it to help answer more difficult questions.

**Are we investigating the right problem?** Before proposing solutions, it should help distinguish a symptom from a potential underlying cause.

**What evidence supports this hypothesis?** It should make uncertainty visible rather than turn weak signals into confident recommendations.

**What might change my mind?** It should help explore alternative explanations instead of simply reinforcing the first plausible story.

**What should I investigate next?** Its analysis should respond to new context and human insights, rather than repeatedly producing recommendations from the same initial assumptions.

**What did the experiment teach me?** The result should inform the next decision, including a decision to abandon the original hypothesis.

These are not features that automatically produce better growth. Their value depends on the quality of the underlying data, the way the system behaves, and whether they actually lead to better decisions.

But they're the questions I'd use to evaluate whether an AI growth tool is helping me think better, rather than merely helping me produce more.

## The opportunity beyond AI-powered growth

I don't think the future of growth belongs to whoever generates the most experiments or automates the most marketing tasks.

Those capabilities will continue to matter, but they may become easier for more teams to access.

The more interesting advantage could come from understanding customers better, asking more useful questions, challenging assumptions earlier, and learning from evidence more effectively.

AI can contribute significantly to that process. It can analyze information at scale, explore alternatives, and uncover patterns that a person might miss. Human judgment can provide context, recognize what matters, and redirect the investigation when the initial approach is wrong.

Neither contribution is sufficient on its own in every situation, and combining them doesn't guarantee a better outcome.

The challenge is to design the interaction so that each contribution improves the next step.

**For me, the opportunity isn't simply to use AI to execute growth faster. It's to figure out how human and AI intelligence can combine to discover better opportunities, make better decisions, and learn what actually drives growth.**
